For the ones who keep the light on.

Chapter OneThe Visitor

The storm arrived without permission, the way most things did on Ashpoint Point. Mara counted the seconds between lightning and thunder, and decided she had time to make tea before the world grew loud.

The kettle had been her mother's; the tea was not. Everything else on the island still belonged to the woman who had painted the kitchen that particular shade of yellow, back when the ceiling was bare and the two of them had nothing better to do than argue about names for the boat.

•••

By the time the knock came, Mara had already decided how the next hour would go. She would say very little. She would learn everything.

"You're the keeper's daughter," the man said, when the door opened. Water ran off his coat in neat, organized rivers. He held a briefcase like a suitcase, as if he had practiced for both.

"I'm the keeper," Mara said, and did not invite him in. "My father liked the title better."

Chapter TwoThe Tower

The tower was one hundred and eleven steps, and Mara took them every morning whether or not she wanted to look at the sea. Wanting had stopped being part of the routine years ago. Routine was the thing that wanted.

At the top, the lamp room was small and warm and smelled of brass polish. She wiped the glass on days she needed to think, which was most days now, and found that the work of keeping something clean was a quiet argument against chaos.

•••

"Do you ever leave?" the man from the mainland asked, the first time he climbed up with her. He had decided, apparently, that he was allowed.

"If I leave," Mara said, "who will tell the light to keep going?"

He laughed. She did not. After a while he stopped laughing too, and looked out at the water as if it had just told him something.

Chapter ThreeThe Letters

The letters were in a biscuit tin her father had used for screws and small useful sins. There were nine of them. Each one was addressed to Mara, in ink so steady it looked printed. Each one had never been sent.

She read the first on the stairs, sitting where the hundredth step always wobbled. She read the last one standing at the top, which is different, because standing up means you can look out while you take bad news in.

•••

The man from the mainland came back with pastries the color of October. He ate two, apologized for both, and asked a question he must have been saving, because it came out whole.

"Why didn't he ever mail them?"

Mara set down the ninth letter, which was the shortest, and for a moment was twenty years away, in that yellow kitchen, arguing about the boat.

Chapter FourThe Crossing

The mainland ferry came twice a week, if it came at all. Mara had not taken it in four years, not since the hospital trips were over and the schedule of leaving had flattened back into the schedule of staying.

But there was a crate in the boathouse with her name on it, and a ferry that left at noon, and a bulb for the lamp room that would not buy itself.

•••

The sea, as always, had opinions about the plan. The boat arrived late, full of grocery boxes and one very calm dog. The dog walked up to Mara without hesitation, sat down, and leaned against her shin like it had important news.

"Falor," read the tag on his collar. "He likes lighthouses," said his owner, arriving at a run. "He stands and stares at them. We have to book cabins facing one."

Mara looked at the island, which was turning gold in the late light, and then at the dog, who had apparently made a decision on her behalf. She respected that. It was her own favorite method.
