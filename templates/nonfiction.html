Introduction: AI, Explained Like a Friend Would

This book assumes zero experience. You do not need to be "technical." If you can send an email, you already have every skill this book builds on.

Artificial intelligence has a reputation problem. Half the world talks about it like magic, and the other half talks about it like a courtroom. Both voices are loud. Neither is very useful when you just want to get something done before dinner.

So we will do something unglamorous: we will use the tools, carefully, one step at a time, and keep noticing what they are actually good at. By the end of the first chapter you will have used an AI assistant for three real tasks, and you will be able to explain to a friend exactly where it helped and where it didn't.

Tip

There are no wrong questions in this book. If a term confuses you, that is the author's problem, not yours. Every term gets defined the first time it appears.

Chapter 1: What AI Actually Is

Nine pages of definition could be replaced by one honest sentence, so here it is: an AI assistant is very good at guessing what words usually come next.

That sounds small. It is not small. Most of writing, summarizing, translating, and explaining is exactly that: producing language that fits what came before. The surprise of the last few years is how far a machine can get with that one skill.

But knowing the limitation matters more than the party trick. A language model does not "know" your bank balance, your medical history, or whether the store is open on Sunday. It knows what people usually write, and sometimes what people usually write is wrong.

1.1 The three things it does well

  • Summarizing. Give it a long, boring document and it will hand back the short version, usually accurately.
  • Explaining. Ask it to explain anything at a level you choose, from "explained to a friend" down to "explained to me like I have five minutes and no patience."
  • Drafting. Emails, outlines, lists of questions for a doctor's appointment, recipes from the contents of your fridge.

1.2 The two things it does badly

  1. Facts without a source. It will answer confidently even when nobody wrote the answer down anywhere.
  2. Math with many steps. It gets better every year, and it is still reasonable to double-check anything important with a calculator.

"Treat an AI answer like advice from a smart friend who has read a lot but slept very little lately: often useful, occasionally nonsense, always worth a second look on anything that matters."

Chapter 2: Asking Better Questions

The single habit that separates people who get good results from people who get blamed results: tell the AI who it is, what it is helping with, and what "good" looks like.

Compare these two requests. "Write something about snakes" will produce something generic enough to forget by dinner. But this next request produces something you can actually use:

Write a 5-sentence explanation of why snakes shed their skin,
for a curious 8-year-old. Use one comparison to something
a kid has seen, like socks. No puns.

Length, audience, tone, and one example to copy. That is the whole recipe, and it works in every AI tool you will ever open.

2.1 A tiny prompt pattern you can reuse forever

IngredientExampleWhy it helps
Role"You are a friendly writing coach" Sets the tone and vocabulary
Task"make my email kind but firm" One job, clearly named
Audience"for my landlord" Controls formality
Format"under 120 words" Keeps results usable
Note

The pattern is not magic. It just removes guesswork, and removing guesswork is most of what we call "being good with AI."

Chapter 3: Your First Week With AI

Do not start with a grand project. Start with seven small, slightly annoying chores and let the tool carry one of them each day.

  • Monday: summarize a long email thread before answering it.
  • Tuesday: draft the thank-you note that you have owed since March.
  • Wednesday: turn the week's appointments into a one-line schedule.
  • Thursday: ask for three meal ideas from what's actually in the fridge.
  • Friday: have it explain one word from the news like a friend would.
  • Saturday: plan tomorrow's errands in the order you would truly do them.
  • Sunday: notice one place the AI was wrong, and smile — that noticing is the whole skill.

After a week you will have an honest, personal sense of what this technology does for someone exactly like you, which is worth more than ten books of predictions, including this one.
